\section{Introduction}
Jev brings renewed attention to models that answer specified questions with typed decisions rather than generated text \citep{almeida2026jev}. This interface is a natural candidate for biological sequence analysis: many downstream tasks, including promoter detection and structural-class prediction, ask which label applies to a sequence. A useful model should combine the sequence with the question being asked, reuse parameters across tasks, and return a label that downstream software can identify unambiguously. These requirements motivate a biological decision model, while leaving sequence generation, continuous prediction, and structural reconstruction outside the present scope.

Two common approaches address different parts of this problem. A sequence encoder with a classification head directly predicts a valid class, but its output coordinates are tied to a predefined label inventory. Multiple heads can share one encoder; a separate full model per task is not necessary. Sequence--language models instead use task instructions and answer templates to expose multiple biological tasks through a shared language interface \citep{wang2024llamagene,almeida2025chatnt}. With unconstrained generation, however, an answer may paraphrase a label, return several labels, or leave the requested set. These are possible interface failures to measure, not evidence that every generative biological model exhibits them. Constrained decoding can also enforce an output contract \citep{scholak2021picard}.

Our central question is whether a \emph{Jev-style candidate decision interface} offers a useful middle ground: task descriptions and candidate meanings are supplied as input, while the output remains a distribution over the supplied candidates. We use ``Jev-style'' to describe this interface, not to claim reproduction of TypeSafe's model or training algorithm. Laya-Bio adapts the open Laya encoder \citep{laya2026}, using one shared candidate scorer for both DNA and protein tasks. An index selects the exact supplied label, so the model cannot invent a new output label. It can still select the wrong candidate, misinterpret the question, or change its decision when the same labels are reordered.

This distinction organizes the study into three questions: \textbf{output validity}, \textbf{predictive quality}, and \textbf{task-conditioned reuse}. The current experiment tests joint supervised reuse on two tasks and compares candidate scoring with a shared encoder carrying task-specific heads. It also measures calibration and candidate-order sensitivity. Raw-input candidate scoring reaches 91.10\% DNA and 59.97\% protein test accuracy; a representation-matched biological-BPE comparison favors candidate scoring over the implemented fixed-head control. Biological BPE is a secondary representation study, not the central contribution.

We contribute an open biological adaptation of the candidate-decision interface, a reproducible initial comparison, and an evaluation design that separates label-set compliance from semantic correctness and transfer. The completed experiments do not compare free and constrained biological text generation or establish unseen-task generalization. Appendix~\ref{sec:prospective} specifies those decisive follow-up comparisons, including controls that can also enforce valid labels. Thus the paper presents an initial biological decision-model study and a concrete route to testing its broader scope.

The expanded prospective design connects Choice to exclusive categories, Noul to binary propositions and multilabel panels, and Score to ordered biological measurements. It covers both single and paired sequences. These interfaces motivate a broader task suite; the present results remain a two-task Choice study and do not establish the proposed extensions.
